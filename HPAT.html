Hand Paralysis Assistive Technology

2026–2027 · Biola University · Senior Project IN PROGRESS
Mechatronics Assistive Technology Control Systems Sensors & Actuators

Overview

After a stroke, the muscles that close the hand can stay constantly active while the muscles that open it barely respond, leaving the hand curled in a fist. Our client, a stroke survivor, found existing splints and spring-loaded gloves too bulky, heavy, and difficult to put on with one hand. Working with him and Biola's occupational therapy faculty, our team of three engineers is building two devices: a static splint that holds the fingers open overnight and during rest, and a powered exoskeleton glove that assists grasping and releasing during daily tasks. My focus is researching actuation, sensing, and control options for the powered device.

Approach

We moved from client interviews and OT consultations through a House of Quality, morphological charts, and a weighted decision matrix to select a concept for each device. Both are designed to be put on with one hand, weigh well under 3.5 lb, and avoid pressure on the palm, which triggers the muscles that close the hand. The splint uses individual finger cuffs on a modular frame with magnetic attachment points. The powered glove uses an exoskeleton frame with a rigid linkage that can drive both opening and closing, servo motors, and a planned PID controller. We chose servos over pneumatics because the pneumatic systems in the literature required control boxes of 1.6 to 2 kg. The power switch also serves as an emergency stop by disconnecting the battery from the motors.

Progress So Far

We have defined requirements and applicable medical device standards, surveyed existing devices and research, and selected initial concepts for both products. We are now preparing a midterm design review and starting rough prototypes. Early findings changed our plans: the client's opening muscles barely fire, so EMG control may be unreliable, and industry feedback pushed us to test the linkage mechanically before adding motors and electronics.

Next Steps

The goal is a client- and OT-approved splint by mid-December and a rough powered prototype by the end of the year. In spring we will refine the powered glove through CAD, fabrication, controls tuning, and client testing, finishing in April.
