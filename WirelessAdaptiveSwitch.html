Wireless Adaptive Switch

Summer 2024 · Biola University
Circuit Troubleshooting Soldering

Overview

Individuals with disabilities or neurodiversity (e.g. cerebral palsy, autism, Down syndrome) can have difficulty engaging with toys and other devices due to physical or cognitive challenges. Adaptive switches broaden access by giving users a large, easy-to-activate interface they can press with a hand, foot, or even their head. Commercial adaptive switches typically cost $250–$300, and cheaper wired alternatives limit range and device compatibility. We were tasked with designing a wireless, low-cost adaptive switch accessible to pediatric, adult, and geriatric clients, compatible with a wide range of battery-operated devices. The project also served an occupational-therapy training purpose and was field-tested in a missions setting in Baja California, Mexico.

Approach

I built and tested the first prototype using an IR transmitter/receiver pair, a common low-cost method for short-range wireless on/off control. During testing with the actual battery-operated toys we targeted, the on command signal was recieved, but not the off-command singal. Troubleshooting traced the problem to the toys' own DC motor noise that was interfering with the IR communication. It could easily recieve an IR command when the toy was off, but once the motors started, the off command was lost in the motor nosie. I tested using an RF communication frequency instead which solved the problem and the final design uses a 433 MHz RF link between transmitter (switch) and receiver (attached to the toy).

To connect the RF receiver without modifying a toy's internal wiring, I built a battery interrupter inserted into the toy's existing battery compartment, made of two strips of dual-sided copper foil tape with the receiver's wires sandwiched between the conductive layers. Pressing the switch lets the receiver close the connection, allowing current to flow from battery to device.

Results

Field-tested in Baja California, Mexico — kids engaged easily with the switch. Some connections proved weak under rough handling so I reinforced the battery interrupter by soldering the wires for a more durable, kid-friendly design.
